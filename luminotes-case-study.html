LumiNotes

Designing a clearer way for households to understand their electricity bill, track usage against a budget, and catch problems before the due date — built in a 24-hour university hackathon.

Role
UI/UX Designer
Team
4, cross-functional
Platform
Mobile App
Tools
Figma · FigJam
01

The problem

Electricity bills in most households arrive as a single confusing total — a peso amount and a due date, with the actual usage breakdown buried in fine print or missing entirely. People can't tell what drove the bill up until it's already too late to change anything.

For lower-income households especially, an unexpectedly high bill isn't just an inconvenience — it can mean a missed payment, a late fee, or disconnection. The problem isn't that people don't care about their usage; it's that the bill gives them no way to see it coming.

02

Who it's for

Primary

The household bill-payer

  • Manages the family's monthly budget
  • Checks usage reactively, only after the bill arrives
  • Wants a warning before the bill is a surprise, not a breakdown after
Secondary

The budget-conscious renter

  • Splits utility costs with roommates
  • Needs to explain and justify shared costs
  • Wants usage data simple enough to screenshot and share
03

Goals

Beyond looking clean, the product needed to change behavior: catch a high bill early, not just report it late.

Reduce billing surprises
→
Make usage legible
→
Prompt action before due date
04

Research & insights

With a 24-hour build window, research was fast and targeted: short interviews with five household bill-payers, a teardown of three existing utility apps, and a quick heuristic pass on the physical paper bill itself — which turned out to be most people's only real mental model of "what a bill looks like."

Finding
Every person interviewed could describe last month's bill amount, but none could describe what changed since the month before.
Implication
The single total number was the only thing being communicated — trend and cause were invisible.
Action
Made month-over-month comparison a primary, always-visible element rather than a secondary chart.
Finding
Existing utility apps we tore down all led with account/meter numbers and settings — not the bill itself.
Implication
The information people open the app for was consistently one or two taps deeper than it needed to be.
Action
Put the current bill status, not a dashboard menu, as the home screen's first and only focus.
05

Structure & flow

With three real screens to design in the time available, the structure stayed deliberately flat — everything a bill-payer needs is one tap from home.

Home / Bill Status
→
Usage Breakdown
→
Set Budget Alert
→
Confirmation

No account setup, no settings menu, no onboarding tour — for the hackathon scope, every screen had to earn its place in the flow or get cut.

06

Design exploration

Two directions competed for the home screen: a chart-first dashboard, and a status-first summary. The team evaluated both against the core goal — reducing surprise, not maximizing data density.

Option A — Rejected

A dashboard of usage charts up front. Data-rich, but it asked the user to interpret a graph before telling them whether they were in good shape or not.

Option B — Selected

A plain-language status line first — "On track" or "Higher than usual" — with the chart available underneath for anyone who wants the detail.

07

Key design decisions

Status before data
The home screen answers "am I okay?" in one glance, before it shows a single chart. That's the question every interviewee actually had.
Comparison, not just totals
Every number on the bill breakdown is shown next to last month's equivalent, so a change is visible without doing mental math.
One accent color, used sparingly
A single amber accent is reserved only for "needs attention" states, so it stays meaningful instead of decorative.

10

Outcome

LumiNotes won first place at the university hackathon, with judges specifically calling out the status-first home screen as the strongest differentiator against other billing-app entries.

This was a 24-hour hackathon project — a working, judged prototype rather than a shipped product with live usage data.

11

Reflection

What I learned

Under a hard deadline, cutting scope is a design decision, not a compromise — deciding what the home screen would not try to do was as important as what it would.

What I'd improve

The budget-alert flow was the least tested piece. Given more time, I'd validate the alert threshold-setting interaction with actual bill-payers, not just the team.

What I'd do next

Extend the comparison pattern used on the home screen into a full usage-history view, so trend-spotting isn't limited to a single month-over-month glance.
